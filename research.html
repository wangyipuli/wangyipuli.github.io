Evapotranspiration, from the ground up

Evapotranspiration is the largest term in the terrestrial water budget after precipitation, and almost everything we claim to know about it at global scale rests on a sparse, gappy network of flux towers. I work on the measurement problem itself: how to reconstruct continuous in-situ records when instruments fail, and how to turn what the network does capture into datasets other people can actually benchmark against.

How the land surface divides its energy

Of the net radiation a surface absorbs, the fraction that leaves as latent rather than sensible heat determines how a landscape buffers heat and drought. I am interested in where that partitioning is shifting, and whether the cause is atmospheric demand, water supply or the vegetation itself — a question where models disagree with each other more than most summaries admit.

surface energy balance latent heat flux attribution

Machine learning where the physics is known but incomplete

Land-surface fluxes come with real physical constraints and genuinely unresolved processes at the same time. I build deep learning models — pre-trained at scale, fine-tuned on flux sites — to push ET estimation to hourly resolution, and I care about when these models extrapolate honestly and when they are simply memorising the sites they were trained on.

Questions I am working on

  • How much of the disagreement between global ET products is a real difference in physics, and how much is an artefact of how the underlying observations were gap-filled?
  • Can a model pre-trained on hundreds of millions of samples generalise to flux sites and ecosystems it has never seen — or does it only appear to?
  • Where is surface energy partitioning shifting fastest, and is the dominant control atmospheric demand, water availability, or vegetation response?
